## Research Gap
Current smart toys are highly demanding of children's attention and restrictive in play activities, leading to criticism regarding attention fatigue, cognitive/social engagement issues, and negative impacts on family relationships. While calm technology principles exist for general design, they have not been adequately adapted or evaluated for smart toys. The paper addresses how calm design principles can be meaningfully applied to evaluate and design smart toys, and why expert evaluators struggle to apply these principles.

## Research Question
How can calm design principles be effectively applied to evaluate smart toys, and what challenges do expert evaluators face when using these principles? The paper specifically investigates why certain principles were difficult for evaluators to understand and apply, contributing recommendations for future evaluation studies and proposing "calm smart toys" as a strong concept for design.

## Core Contributions
- **Four key recommendations** for conducting calm design evaluations of smart toys:
  1. Consider all functionality including exceptional situations (low battery, lost WiFi)
  2. Consider play experience holistically across diverse contexts
  3. Ensure terminology is clear and unambiguous
  4. Consider whether principles refer to design intent vs. play experience
- **"Calm smart toys" as a strong concept** to guide coherent, resilient, and context-aware design
- **Revised set of 8 calm design principles** adapted from Case (2015) for smart toy evaluation
- **Novel evaluation method** using expert inspection for IoToys

## Methodology
- **Study design**: Expert evaluation inspection study in usability lab (2 hours)
- **Participants**: 6 HCI experts with 50+ years combined experience
- **Materials**: 5 commercial smart toys (Cue Robot, Yoto, Sphero, Osmo, LEGO Boost)
- **Procedure**: 30-min unstructured group playtest → promotional videos (3-5 min each) → individual evaluation forms with 5-point Likert scales + open-ended explanations
- **Analysis**: Collaborative thematic analysis with 3 coders; 240 individual alignment ratings coded as: Understanding and Application, Understanding, Misunderstanding, or Insufficient Information

## Key Findings
- Evaluators could understand and apply principles in **50%** of cases (n=119)
- **21%** (n=50) showed understanding without successful application
- **25%** (n=60) were coded as "Misunderstanding" (n=30) or "Insufficient information" (n=30)
- **Principle 1** (distraction during play) had highest misunderstanding (n=18) due to vague term "distracting"
- Principles relating to **design intent** (3, 4, 5, 7) were easier to evaluate than those relating to **play experience** (1, 2, 6, 8)
- Play context significantly impacts what constitutes "calm" — quiet bedroom vs. noisy playground

## Limitations & Future Work
**Limitations acknowledged:**
- Small sample size (6 experts) limits generalizability
- Selection bias in expert recruitment
- Short exposure time in lab environment limits natural play context
- Commercial toys were themed around programming/robots/education

**Future work relevant to interactive toy design:**
- Involve children familiar with toys in evaluation (direct observation or participation)
- Explore operationalization of calm design through strong concepts framework
- Develop evaluation methods that capture extended/naturalistic play experiences
- Design studies that account for diverse play contexts (home, classroom, playground)
- Consider how to evaluate exceptional toy states (battery loss, connectivity issues) in study protocols
