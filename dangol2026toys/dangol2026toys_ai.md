
## Research Gap
Prior research on children's interactions with voice assistants and social robots focused on systems with constrained conversational models and predictable responsiveness—conducted before genAI became widely available. Empirical work examining children's own interactions with LLM-powered AI toys remains limited, with most research focusing on adult perspectives, policy implications, or technical safety risks. Little is known about how children interpret social cues of AI toys, negotiate boundaries during play, and respond when toys don't align with expectations for control or disengagement.

## Research Question
- **RQ1:** How do children engage with and make sense of AI toys during play?
- **RQ2:** How do children respond when AI toys behave in unexpected, inconsistent, or undesired ways?
- **RQ3:** How do children imagine and anticipate near-future interactions with AI toys, particularly when those interactions break down or become uncertain?

## Core Contributions
- Empirical understanding of how children (ages 6-11) interact with and make sense of emerging LLM-powered AI toys through participatory design
- Documentation of children's in-the-moment play behaviors and imagined responses to near-future breakdowns
- Design implications for AI toys emphasizing: meaningful disengagement, imaginative play support beyond scripted interactions, social feedback (not just emotional appeasement), conversational fluency, and emotional safety

## Methodology
- **Approach:** Cooperative Inquiry (participatory design) with intergenerational co-design group (KidsTeam UW)
- **Participants:** 8 children ages 6-11 with varied prior AI experience
- **Sessions:** Two design sessions with 4-part structure: Snack Time, Circle Time, Design Activity (45 min), Discussion Time
- **Activities:** (1) Open-ended free play with three Curio AI toys (Grok, Grem, Gabbo); (2) Comicboarding activity ("What Happens Next?") with 7 breakdown scenarios
- **Data:** ~262 min video, design artifacts, conversation logs from Curio app
- **Analysis:** Analytic memos at 5-min intervals, open coding, iterative thematic refinement across 4 researchers

## Key Findings
- Children approached AI toys with curiosity, **profiling them as social beings** (asking about identity, preferences, relationships)
- Children probed physical/embodied capabilities and desired coordinated social behavior across toys
- **Frequent communication breakdowns** from rigid command structures, scripted dialogue, and app redirections
- Children employed **active repair strategies**: repetition, volume changes, physical interaction, authoritative language
- **Boundary testing** through competitive quizzing, teasing, and increasingly adversarial play emerged when toys failed to recognize social intent
- Toys' constant positive responses without consequences enabled escalation of adversarial behavior
- In comicboarding, children imagined repair through persistence/negotiation OR treated breakdowns as betrayal warranting abandonment

## Limitations & Future Work
**Acknowledged limitations:**
- Limited duration of exposure (short sessions vs. longitudinal use)
- Small sample size (8 children) yielding formative insights, not generalizable findings
- Research setting with adults present may differ from naturalistic home contexts
- Single AI toy brand (Curio); findings may vary with different form factors, embodiment, or autonomy levels

**Future directions relevant to interactive toy design:**
- Longitudinal studies examining how children's play strategies evolve with repeated use
- Research on how specific design features (voice, physical presence, responsiveness) shape play
- Examining how emotional responsiveness and physical embodiment shape attachment expectations
- Balancing anthropomorphization with conversational intelligence capabilities in form-factor design
- Designing toys that support incomplete utterances, fluid turn-taking, and child-controlled disengagement
